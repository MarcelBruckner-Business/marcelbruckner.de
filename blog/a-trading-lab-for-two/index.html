A trading lab for two

A friend and I wanted somewhere to try out trading ideas together: European, US and Asian stocks, some crypto, and no real money. Two people, two laptops, one place where the code, the data and the results live. This is how that place came together on my homelab, what didn’t work the way we expected, and what we have now.

What we tried first

The lab started with OpenTerminalUI, a terminal for market data. It asked its data providers for prices every five seconds, around the clock, which my DNS filter flagged as suspicious, and it mostly knew Indian and US markets. For two European investors it was the wrong tool, so it went.

What stayed from that start is OpenBB as the data source: one API in front of Yahoo Finance and about 30 other providers. Our notebooks fetch prices from it, and Claude can too, through an MCP connector.

What the lab is made of

  • JupyterLab is the one place we write code, a shared server whose work folder is a Git repository we both push to.
  • NautilusTrader tests ideas on stocks right inside a notebook: fetch prices from OpenBB, define a strategy, see the result under the cell.
  • Freqtrade runs crypto bots around the clock, watched through its web interface, FreqUI. Only with play money: no exchange keys, and the deploy fails if anyone turns paper trading off.
  • TradingAgents lets a few LLM “analysts” debate one stock and give a rating. That one costs real API money, so it has a small monthly limit.
  • Claude in the notebook: JupyterLab’s chat, Jupyter AI, talks to Claude through an OpenAI-compatible gateway on my own subscription (ocp), with a key just for the lab. It explains cells, writes strategies and adds cells to the open notebook.

The starter notebook tests SAP with a simple rule: buy when the 10-day average crosses above the 30-day one, sell when it crosses back.

Everyone signs in with one account on the lab’s own domain; how that works without opening the rest of my homelab is in the sign-in post.

What didn’t work the way we expected

A bot is not what we thought. We expected to open FreqUI, add a bot, pick a strategy and go. In Freqtrade, one running program is one bot, with one config, one strategy and one wallet. FreqUI’s “login to your bot” window doesn’t create anything; it saves a connection to a bot that already runs, and it saves it in the browser. And a bot that trades can’t backtest: that needs a second Freqtrade in another mode.

The last step needed me. Moving a tested strategy onto the running bot meant copying a file and restarting a container, so my friend would have had to ask me for the most interesting part of the whole lab.

What we have now

Two bots, and one folder for each:

  • Backtest sits idle until you pick a strategy in FreqUI and click start; then it replays past prices in about a minute. Its folder, backtest/, holds every experiment.
  • Paper-Trading runs one strategy all day with play money. Its folder, paper-trading/, holds only what runs.
flowchart LR
  T[Template strategy] -->|duplicate| E[Your strategy in backtest/]
  E --> BB[Backtest bot: past prices, on click]
  BB -->|good enough| TO[Notebook: take it over]
  TO --> PB[Paper-Trading bot: live prices, play money]
  BB --> UI[FreqUI]
  PB --> UI

The workflow:

  1. In backtest/, duplicate the template strategy and rename it.

  2. Change it and test it in the Backtest bot. Freqtrade’s example strategy over the last month made 24 trades and turned 10,000 into 7,551 USDT of play money. It’s an example, not a suggestion.

  3. When it’s good, take it over yourself: a notebook lists the strategies, you write one file name and press run. It has the Backtest bot load the strategy for real first, so a broken one is refused before anything changes. Then it copies it in, reloads the Paper-Trading bot and waits until it runs again. If it doesn’t come back, the previous strategy is put back.

FreqUI opens with both bots already there, because a small start page fills in what FreqUI would otherwise ask for. JupyterLab opens on the repository’s README, with a diagram of which folder belongs to which tool, and every folder has its own README. The lab’s wiki has one page per workflow plus a page of links for learning Python and Jupyter.

What I took from it

  • Learn the tool’s model before naming things. Our folders are named after the bots now, because a strategy belongs to one bot.
  • If someone has to ask you to do a step, that step isn’t finished.
  • No real money until it’s a separate, deliberate decision. Everything here is backtests and paper trading, and the setup makes sure it stays that way.
